Research/Training Dynamics

A model is a process caught at one moment

To understand why a model behaves as it does, we study the trajectory that produced it: how data, architecture, optimization, scale, and variation across runs shape what the model learns.

AI needs explanations of development, not just descriptions of outcomes

Most AI research examines a finished model and responds to undesirable behavior with another round of fine-tuning, filtering, or prompting. These interventions can be useful without explaining why the behavior emerged, whether the fix will survive, or how the behavior could have been prevented during training.

A science of AI should make claims before the final model is observed. It should identify which properties are stable across runs, explain how mechanisms form, and connect changes in the training process to changes in behavior.

Position: Don’t Just “Fix it in Post”A Science of AI Must Study Training Dynamics Biderman, Khan, Mireshghallah, Arnett, Barez, Saphra ICML 2026, Oral ↗

An observatory for model development

Longitudinal science requires more than final weights. Pythia was designed so researchers can reconstruct what each model saw, observe its development at high temporal resolution, and make controlled comparisons across scale. PolyPythias adds repeated runs, allowing stable phenomena to be separated from seed-specific accidents.

Mechanisms have histories

A circuit found in a finished model may be a stable algorithm, temporary scaffolding, or a vestige of an earlier strategy. Following circuits through training shows that task-level algorithms can persist across time and scale even as the individual components implementing them change.

Learning has an order

Networks do not acquire every regularity at once. They first exploit simpler statistics and later become sensitive to higher-order structure. Some early capabilities are eventually displaced or reorganized, making the path itself necessary evidence for an explanation.

Trajectories contain predictive information

Intermediate checkpoints can reveal which behaviors a model is likely to exhibit at the end of training. Memorization is a demanding test: the goal is not merely to forecast an aggregate score, but to predict which individual training sequences a larger model will later reproduce.

Questions that organize the program

The aim is not a catalogue of curves. We want causal, quantitative accounts that constrain what we expect from new training runs and explain which observations should generalize.

Which properties of a final model can be forecast from its early trajectory?

Which mechanisms recur across seeds, scales, architectures, and datasets?

What determines the order in which capabilities and representations develop?

When can undesirable behavior be prevented during training rather than patched afterward?
